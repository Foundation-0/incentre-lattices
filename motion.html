04 // MOTION

Motion

The dots’ touching balls form a framework that can move. Rotate the squares of 4.8.8 balls alternately clockwise and anticlockwise and the whole pattern contracts in both directions at once, a negative Poisson ratio, until at 45° every ball touches four neighbours and the pattern has become the square packing. Among the dot positions scanned, only the incentre ends in the regular square lattice. The same idea in 3D, with the lattice held cubic, reaches a six-contact state at 60°. This page derives the numbers, shows the fold moving, and then counts how many other motions the framework has.

1The framework

Treat each ball as a joint, and each touching pair as a rigid bar of length 2R = 2√2 − 2 joining their centres. The bars can swivel freely at the joints, as the pieces of a model made from rods and pins can. This is a pin-jointed framework. In the plane, the four dots of one square are joined by four bars, and neighbouring squares are joined by one bar through the shared edge, along an axis. In 3D, the six dots of one cube form an octahedron of twelve bars, and neighbouring octahedra are joined by one bar through the shared face.

A motion that keeps every bar its length is a mechanism. The structure is not rigid if it has one. A square of four bars is not rigid (it can shear into a diamond), but an octahedron with all its triangular faces is. A group of dots that moves as one stiff body is called a cluster: the 4-dot square in 2D, the 6-dot octahedron in 3D.

2The 2D fold

Rotate the clusters in a checkerboard pattern: each cluster by +θ if it is on an even square of the board, and by −θ if on an odd one. Take a cluster at the origin with its dots at distance t from its centre along the axes, and the cross bar from its +x dot to the −x dot of the neighbour whose centre is at (a, 0). The +x dot of the first, rotated by θ, is at (t cos θ, t sin θ). The −x dot of the second, rotated by −θ, is at (a − t cos θ, +t sin θ). The two heights are equal, so the bar stays horizontal: it stays on the axis. Its length is a − 2t cos θ. The bar must keep its original length L = 2 − 2t, so the spacing between clusters must be

a(θ) = L + 2t cos θ = 2 − 2t (1 − cos θ)

Every bar length is preserved exactly. This is a finite motion of the whole framework, not only an infinitesimal one: in the code the bar-length error stays below 10−15 along the path. The spacing shrinks equally in x and y, so the strain is isotropic, εxx = εyy = a/2 − 1, and the Poisson ratio, which is minus the ratio of the sideways strain to the pulling strain, is −1 along the whole path.PROVEN For small θ the spacing is a ≈ 2 − tθ²: the contraction starts at second order, so to first order the straight lattice still resists being squeezed, and the fold exists as a finite mechanism.

3Where it locks, and the incentre

The balls have radius R = √2 − 1 when t = 2 − √2. As the fold proceeds, balls from different clusters approach. The gap between the balls of neighbouring clusters along the bar axis is 2√2 t cos(θ + 45°), which first reaches zero at exactly θ = 45°.PROVEN That no other pair of non-bonded balls touches earlier is checked numerically on a 0.01° grid, not proved.VERIFIED NUMERICALLY Put t = 2 − √2 in the formula for the spacing:

a(45°) = 2 − 2(2 − √2)(1 − √2/2) = 2 − 2(3 − 2√2) = 4√2 − 4 = 4R

Rotated by 45°, each dot sits at (±t/√2, ±t/√2) = (±R, ±R) from its cluster’s centre. Clusters are 4R apart, so the dots form a square lattice of spacing 2R. Every ball touches 4 others (it touched 3 before). The consequences, all exact:PROVEN (symbolic check in research/mechanics/exact_checks.py; numerics in mechanism.py)

  • The linear shrink factor is a/2 = 2R = 2(√2 − 1) = 0.8284.
  • The strain in each direction is a/2 − 1 = −(√2 − 1)² = −R² = −0.1716.
  • The area of a cell shrinks by the factor 4R² = 12 − 8√2 = 0.6863.
  • The packing density, the fraction of the plane the balls cover, rises from π(√2 − 1)² = 0.5390 to π/4 = 0.7854, the density of the square packing.

Now move the dot to another position t on the axis. At 45° the framework has two bar lengths, √2 t inside a cluster and 2 − 2t across, and they are equal only when √2 t = 2 − 2t, which is t = 2 − √2.PROVEN Whether the fold really ends at 45° for other t was checked on a scan of six values of t (0.45, 0.5, 0.55, 2 − √2, 0.62, 0.7) with a 0.01° grid. For t = 0.62 and 0.7 the fold locks at 45° with two different contact distances. For t below 2 − √2 it does not lock at 45°: the fold continues and locks at 66.37° (t = 0.45), 56.96° (0.5) and 49.51° (0.55), with three contacts per ball. So among the values scanned, only the incentre ends in the regular square lattice, where the “uniform” start state folds onto the “uniform” end state. 4The 3D fold

In three dimensions the clusters are octahedra, coloured in a 3D checkerboard. Even octahedra are rotated by R and odd ones by its inverse, about an axis of your choice. The cross bars again stay on the axes, and the lattice spacing along each axis is chosen so that every bar keeps its length. Three axes give three different endings. 5How many other motions are there? Counting floppy modes

The fold is one finite motion. To find all the small motions, count. Each of the 2n dots in a cell can move in n directions, giving 2n² freedoms per cell. Each bar takes one away. Per cell there are 2n(n − 1) bars inside the cluster (every pair of dots except antipodes) plus n cross bars, 2n² − n in all. So the freedoms exceed the bars by n per cell. This is the counting of Maxwell and Calladine: floppy modes minus self-stresses equals freedoms minus bars, here n per cell.PROVEN (arithmetic) A floppy mode is a way to move the dots that keeps every bar length unchanged (to first order). A state of self-stress is a way to put tension and compression on the bars so that every dot is in force balance with nothing pushing from outside. The split between the two counts needs its own proof, given below.

The result for a generic wavevector (that is, for a wavelike motion that repeats from cell to cell with a generic phase) is that there are exactly n(n−1)/2 floppy modes per cell and n(n−3)/2 states of self-stress, which is 0 in 3D, 2 in 4D, 5 in 5D and 9 in 6D. The floppy modes are the small rotations of the cross-polytopes (the octahedra in 3D), and they cost no energy at any wavelength: their frequencies form flat bands.PROVEN for n ≥ 3, VERIFIED NUMERICALLY for n = 2 to 6 (singular-value rank, tolerance 10−8, several random wavevectors).

The proof, for n ≥ 3: the skeleton of a cross-polytope is infinitesimally rigid, because it is a simplicial polytope (Cauchy, Dehn, Alexandrov, Whiteley). So in a zero mode each cluster moves as a stiff body: a translation plus a rotation. The cross bar along axis i lies on the line joining the two cluster centres, so the rotation drops out of its length change, and the bar condition reduces to a condition on the translations alone. In wave form it reads (e2iqi − 1)vi = 0, which forces the translation to vanish at a generic wavevector. Only the n(n−1)/2 rotations are left. In 2D the argument fails because a 4-bar square is not rigid: there are 2 zero modes per cell and no self-stress. On each plane qi = 0 there is one extra mode (rigid sliding of rows along axis i).

The counts do not depend on where the dot sits on the axis, for any t between 0 and 1. The incentre does not change the mode count.PROVEN

In 3D this is the Prussian-blue topology of the previous page, and Goodwin’s 2006 paper reports its density of rigid unit modes.KNOWN The formula for every n and the self-stress count n(n−3)/2: no prior reference found.NO REFERENCE FOUND (web searches only)

Which strains does the lattice allow at first order? In n ≥ 3 the mechanisms produce exactly the n(n−1)/2 pure shears and every normal strain is blocked. In 2D they produce the two-dimensional space of shape-changing strains without area change, so only the bulk modulus is non-zero: a fluid-like, 2D pentamode material.VERIFIED NUMERICALLY

6How stiff is it? The Poisson ratio

Put a unit spring on every bar and stretch the lattice along axis 1. The Poisson ratio ν100 is minus the sideways strain divided by the stretching strain. Using the symmetric relaxation in which vertices move only along their own axes, the elastic constants per unit volume are

C11 = 4(n − 1)² / (n² 2n) C12 = 4 / (n² 2n) C44 = 0

which gives ν100 = C12/(C11 + (n−2)C12) = 1/(n² − n − 1). The general-n step is a linear solve with si = (2εi − Σε/n)/n, and the numerics agree for n = 2 to 8.PROVEN (every n ≥ 3; the same algebra gives ν = 1 formally at n = 2) In 3D, ν = 1/5 exactly, and it does not depend on t for unit springs. For bars of the same material (stiffness proportional to 1/L), ν(t) rises monotonically from 0.035 to 0.32, and its value at the incentre equals 1/5 only because all bars there have equal length: the incentre is not an extremum.VERIFIED NUMERICALLY No prior reference found for these closed forms.NO REFERENCE FOUND (web searches only)

7A census of 159 frameworks, and pentamodes

The dot is one case of a general recipe, the Wythoff construction of the mirrors page, applied to the mirror groups of tilings and honeycombs: the nine affine Coxeter groups C̃2, G̃2, Ã2, C̃3, B̃3, Ã3, C̃4, B̃4 and D̃4, with every choice of which mirrors the generating point lies on. That is 159 frameworks, every nonempty ringing of the nine diagrams. For each, the survey compared the uniform point (the incentre-type point, equidistant from the mirrors it is not on) with a random point on the same face of the chamber. The mode and self-stress counts were identical in every case: at generic wavevectors, at q = 0, and in the dimension of the lattice strains the framework can reach.VERIFIED NUMERICALLY How much this shows needs care. In the 36 single-ringed cases the face is a single corner of the chamber, so the random point is the uniform point and agreement is automatic. Rings related by a symmetry of the diagram are counted separately (for C̃2, rings (0) and (2) have identical rows in the census file). The non-trivial comparison is the 123 multi-ringed cases, and counting each symmetry class once leaves 70 of them (91 classes in all). Moving the dot off the incentre, without changing which mirrors it lies on, never changes the linear mechanics. The incentre extremises packing quantities, not mode counts. It is mechanically ordinary, apart from the fold ending described above.

Two patterns appear in the census.

  • Pentamodes. Every framework whose vertices have z = n + 1 neighbours (all 2D truncated and omnitruncated tilings; in 3D the omnitruncated honeycombs and C̃3 rings (1,2) and (1,2,3); in 4D the z = 5 cases) has no self-stress at a generic wavevector and exactly one at q = 0. The strains it can reach form a space of dimension n(n + 1)/2 − 1: only dilation is blocked. That is a pentamode material in 3D (five soft modes and one stiff), and a bulk-only material in 2D. For the omnitruncated frameworks the single stress is exact: tension proportional to the area of the facet of the chamber that each bar crosses is in equilibrium at every vertex, by Minkowski’s relation Σ|Fi|ni = 0.PROVEN (uniqueness of the stress: VERIFIED NUMERICALLY) The pentamode principle itself, junctions of z = n + 1 bars with one stress, is known (Milton and Cherkaev 1995; Kadic and others 2012).KNOWN Its systematic appearance across all Wythoff omnitruncations: no census found.NO REFERENCE FOUND (web searches only)
  • Flat-band Maxwell cases. The cantellated cubic C̃3 ring (0,2), C̃4 (1,3), C̃4 (0,2,4), B̃4 (1,3,4) and D̃4 (0,1,3,4) have z = 2n, but equal numbers of zero modes and self-stresses at every wavevector. They behave like the pyrochlore and kagome cases (Ã3 (0,2), G̃2 (1)), which are known to sit on the Maxwell line.KNOWN

No compact formula in n covers the whole census beyond the contact family above and the z = n + 1 family, where the number of generic zero modes per cell is nV − E = (n − 1)|W/T|/2 for omnitruncations. No census of the rigidity of uniform honeycombs was found. 8Predictions for a printed version

Suppose balls are printed fused at small necks, so that the necks act as flexible hinges. These predictions follow from the above and have not been tested.CONJECTURE

  • Extreme cubic anisotropy. C44 comes only from neck bending while C11 and C12 come from neck stretching, so C44/C11 should scale like (neck radius / ball radius)². The Zener ratio 2C44/(C11 − C12) should be much less than 1.
  • Tension and compression differ. The axes are straight chains of bars, stiff in tension. Under compression the fold mode has zero first-order cost, so the lattice should buckle into the counter-rotated pattern and switch to ν ≈ −1, the 3D analogue of the buckled holey sheet of Bertoldi and co-workers.
  • In 2D the printed 4.8.8 sheet should start with ν near +1 (bulk-only), then after buckling lock into the square packing at a linear strain of −17.2% (−R²).

9What this does not show

All the 3D tilt results use a 2×2×2 checkerboard with the lattice vectors kept on the axes. Letting the lattice shear is a separate branch of the mechanism; its continuation to hexagonal and cubic close packing (see the Search page) was checked on the 2×2×2 block only, so larger blocks and other stackings are untested. The ball-collision test checks the nearest non-bonded neighbours of one cluster in a block of five cells per side. That is enough for the end states found, but it is not a proof of global non-overlap along the whole path. For 2D the 45° end is proven analytically. The prior-art searches were web searches only, not a full literature review, and the 2D fold in particular may exist as folklore in the rotating-units or kirigami literature.

Most promising open question: map the full configuration space of the ball and bar framework, as a real algebraic variety, for every Wythoff orbit. Which tilt paths are finite? Where do they end in new contact states? Is the incentre always the unique point whose folds end in a uniform packing? The ledger page collects this with the other open questions.
