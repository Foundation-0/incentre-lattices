One abstract drawing, followed all the way down

A pattern of incentres in a square, extended to every dimension, and the places it meets crystal chemistry, sphere packing and rigidity theory.

Ahraz Arifuddin, Aska Digital

Draw a square and its two diagonals, then mark, in each of the four triangles, the incentre: the one point equally far from all three sides, the centre of the inscribed circle. Carried into a cube and into higher dimensions, the drawing lands on known structures: a tiling by squares and octagons, a honeycomb of truncated cubes, the radius-ratio limits of crystal chemistry and the boron framework of calcium hexaboride, all classical (Wythoff, Coxeter, Pauling) and credited as such. What we could not find in print is exact: how rigid the framework of touching balls is in every dimension, one identity behind the radius-ratio limits, a closed form for the densest packing of the boron structure, and a bar-preserving, overlap-free fold of the 3D framework from density 0.2233 to close packing. These come from a web search and two independent checkers, not from peer review, and every claim carries an evidence label.

The radius-ratio identity

The hole-to-ball ratio of the construction equals the classical radius-ratio limit of the polytope the dots form. For the cube, φ = 90° gives √2 − 1.

Holds under stated conditions (mirror walls, or an insphere with equal dihedral angles); the theorem also says where it fails.

The fold, to hexagonal close packing

Every bar length kept, no overlaps, 2×2×2 family, two independent checkers. The end states are easy to verify; the content is the path. Two layer slides then reach fcc (confirmed by two independent checkers).

THE FOLD // REAL DATA
LOOP

The framework (48 balls, 120 bars, one 2×2×2 block with periodic boundaries) deforms continuously through 793 states exported from autoresearch-fold/best/path.npz. Density at each state is exact, from the cell volume. The dashed line at 0.4686 is where the cubic tilt alone stalls; the rest needs the lattice to shear. A geometry result, not a predicted phase transition in real CaB6 or Prussian-blue materials. Prior art: the closest known relative is the ReO3 to VF3 tilt toward hcp (O’Keeffe and Hyde 1977).
